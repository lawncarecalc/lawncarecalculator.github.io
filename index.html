Lawn / Garden Size
⚠ Enter your lawn or garden size so fertilizer quantities calculate correctly on the calculator tabs.

You need to measure just the grass or garden area — not the full lot. Exclude the house, driveway, and any paved areas.

🌎 Method 1 — Google Earth (recommended for irregular lawns)

Google Earth lets you trace your lawn outline directly on satellite imagery and calculates the area automatically. Works well for irregular shapes and areas obscured by trees (use 3D mode to see under tree canopy).

  1. Go to earth.google.com (opens in new tab) and enter your address
  2. Click the ruler icon in the toolbar to open the measure tool
  3. Click around the outside edge of your grass area to drop pins — the area highlights in yellow and updates automatically
  4. Change the units to square feet using the dropdown if needed
  5. For areas under trees, switch to 3D view (lower right corner) to pan around and place pins accurately
  6. Round up to the nearest 100 sq. ft. for a safe estimate

Full step-by-step instructions with screenshots: University of Minnesota Turfgrass Science (opens in new tab)

📍 Method 2 — Google Maps (quick, no download needed)

Google Maps has a built-in measurement tool that works in any browser — no account or app required.

  1. Go to maps.google.com (opens in new tab) and search your address
  2. Switch to Satellite view (bottom left)
  3. Right-click on a corner of your lawn and choose "Measure distance"
  4. Click around the perimeter of your grass area — avoid driveways, beds, and paved areas
  5. Click your starting point again to close the shape — the area appears in the info box in sq. ft.
  6. For multiple separate lawn areas (front and back), measure each separately and add the totals

Tip: zoom in close before placing points for the most accurate outline.

📏 Method 3 — Tape measure (most accurate for simple shapes)

For rectangular or L-shaped lawns a tape measure is fast and precise.

  • Rectangle: length × width = sq. ft.
  • L-shape: divide into two rectangles, calculate each, add together
  • Triangle: ½ × base × height
  • A 100-foot tape measure handles most residential lawns in one pass

🌿 Choosing your annual N rate

Your soil test report may include a nitrogen recommendation — use it as a starting point, but verify it against Virginia guidelines. Waypoint reports sometimes recommend up to 4.0 lbs, which exceeds the VCE limit for cool-season grasses.

The maximum you can apply in a single application depends on how quickly your fertilizer releases nitrogen. Quick-release fertilizers — like urea or ammonium sulfate (Program 1: no "Water Insoluble Nitrogen" or WIN listed on the bag) — have a lower per-application limit. Slow-release fertilizers — look for "Water Insoluble Nitrogen (WIN)" on the bag label (Program 2: 15–49% WIN; Program 3: 50% or more WIN) — allow a higher per-application rate and fewer applications per season. If your annual target is 2.5 lbs and you are using a quick-release product (Program 1, max 0.7 lbs per application for cool-season), you will need at least 4 separate applications to stay within Virginia's limits.

Average lawn — tall fescue / bluegrass / ryegrass2.0–2.5 lbs0.7 (P1) / 0.9 (P2) / 1.5 (P3)3
Higher quality or recovering lawn3.0–3.5 lbs0.7 (P1) / 0.9 (P2) / 1.5 (P3)4

♻ Returning clippings? Recycling grass clippings returns up to one-third of the seasonal nitrogen requirement — about 0.5 to 1 lb of nitrogen per year. If you mulch-mow consistently, reduce your chosen annual total by up to one-third. (VCE 430-011, SPES-384)

☁️ Sun vs. shade: Grasses in heavily shaded areas need only one-half to two-thirds as much nitrogen as the same grass in full sun. Use the lower end of the range above and consider reducing further — over-fertilizing shaded turf promotes disease rather than growth. Time fall applications after most leaves have fallen so sunlight can reach the grass blades. (VCE 430-011)

☁️ ♻ Both shade and returning clippings? Apply both reductions together. Start with the shade adjustment (reduce your target to one-half to two-thirds), then reduce that result by up to one-third for clippings. For example, a shaded tall fescue lawn targeting 2.5 lbs would become 1.25–1.67 lbs after the shade adjustment, then 0.8–1.1 lbs after the clipping credit — a single modest fall application may meet the full seasonal need. (VCE 430-011)

📅 When to apply — think "SON": September, October, November is the primary window for cool-season grasses. Fall applications build density, root growth, and carbohydrate reserves far more effectively than spring fertilization. A single well-timed fall application can meet many lawns' full needs. (VCE 430-520, SPES-670)

🕑 Spring applications (secondary timing): If fall applications were missed or the lawn looks pale green from heavy spring rain leaching, a spring application from April onward is acceptable. Use a lower rate — 0.25 to 0.5 lbs of water-soluble N per 1,000 sq. ft. every 4 to 8 weeks during this period. Avoid heavy spring N applications — they push lush growth at the expense of root development and summer stress tolerance. (SPES-670)

☀️ Choosing your annual N rate

Your soil test report may include a nitrogen recommendation — use it as a starting point, but verify it against Virginia guidelines. Waypoint rates can reach the upper end of the Virginia range — appropriate for bermudagrass but potentially too high for zoysiagrass and centipedegrass. Waypoint's centipedegrass guidance calls for 0.75 to 1 lb N per 1,000 sq. ft. every six weeks through late summer, a derived annual total that can reach approximately 3 lbs — well above the 1 to 2 lb annual ceiling in VCE Soil Test Note 18 (452-718).

The maximum you can apply in a single application depends on how quickly your fertilizer releases nitrogen. Quick-release fertilizers — like urea or ammonium sulfate (Program 1: no "Water Insoluble Nitrogen" or WIN listed on the bag) — have a lower per-application limit. Slow-release fertilizers — look for "Water Insoluble Nitrogen (WIN)" on the bag label (Program 2: 15–49% WIN; Program 3: 50% or more WIN) — allow a higher per-application rate and fewer applications per season. If your annual target is 3.0 lbs and you are using a quick-release product (Program 1, max 0.9 lbs per application for warm-season), you will need at least 4 separate applications to stay within Virginia's limits.

♻ Returning clippings? Recycling grass clippings returns up to one-third of the seasonal nitrogen requirement — about 0.5 to 1 lb of nitrogen per year. Clippings also contribute organic matter that improves soil structure over time. If you mulch-mow consistently, reduce your chosen annual total by up to one-third. (VCE 430-011, SPES-384)

☁️ Sun vs. shade: Warm-season grasses in shade need significantly less nitrogen than the same grass in full sun — use the lower end of the range and reduce frequency. Shaded warm-season grasses are also more susceptible to winterkill, so avoid late-season N applications entirely in shaded areas. (VCE 430-011)

☁️ ♻ Both shade and returning clippings? Apply both reductions together. Start with the shade adjustment (reduce your target to one-half to two-thirds), then reduce that result by up to one-third for clippings. A shaded bermudagrass lawn targeting 3.0 lbs would become 1.5–2.0 lbs after the shade adjustment, then 1.0–1.3 lbs after the clipping credit. For shaded zoysia, the combined result may be well under 1.0 lb annually; for centipede, it may be well under 0.5 lb. (VCE 430-011)

❄️ Potassium and winter hardiness: Potassium is the winterizing nutrient for warm-season grasses. If your soil test rates K as Low, address it in your plan — the benefits of adequate K for winter survival are often more significant for warm-season grasses than for cool-season grasses. If K is already Medium or higher, skip extra K to avoid unnecessary leaching. (VCE 430-520)

📅 When to apply: April 1 through August 15 is the primary window. A modest September application (up to 1 lb water-soluble N per 1,000 sq. ft.) for bermudagrass followed by light applications every 4–6 weeks until frost can extend the growing season and improve winter hardiness — but keep rates conservative and stop before frost. (VCE 430-520)

⛔ Do not fertilize after August 15 unless following the specific fall bermudagrass program above. Late-season nitrogen on warm-season grasses increases winterkill risk.

Vegetable Garden

🌱 Vegetable Garden Calculator

Vegetable crops have very different nitrogen needs — a heavy feeder like tomatoes or corn requires several times more than a light feeder like beans or carrots. A complete N-P-K fertilizer locks all three nutrients into one fixed ratio, so applying enough to meet a crop's nitrogen need almost always means over- or under-supplying phosphorus and potassium at the same time. This tab sizes nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and every other nutrient separately — matched to your crop and your soil test — so each one lands at the right rate instead of riding along with the others.

🔍 Before fertilizing: does your plant need it?

VCE Note 20 recommends fertilization only when one or more of the following symptoms are present:

  • Leaves are paler, smaller, or fewer than normal
  • Plant growth appears stunted or deformed
  • Number of buds or flowers seems reduced
  • Premature fall coloration or leaf drop
  • Reduced twig and branch growth

If the plant is healthy with good green color, no fertilizer is needed. Plants adjacent to a fertilized lawn absorb nutrients from turf applications and generally need no additional supplement. (VCE Note 20)

About and Instructions

🌿

Soil Test Report Assistant

No more guessing what to feed your lawn or garden — a Virginia Cooperative Extension tool for Chesterfield County residents.

An interactive web tool for homeowners in Virginia to interpret soil test results and calculate fertilizer and lime needs for lawns and gardens — built from official VCE publications and research.

📖 How to Use This Calculator

This tool accepts soil test reports from Virginia Cooperative Extension (VCE) / Virginia Tech and Waypoint Analytical. Every calculator starts the same way, on the Soil Test Report tab:

1
Enter your report.
  • Select "What is this report for?" — your lab and what was tested (e.g., "VCE / Virginia Tech — Vegetable Garden").
  • Enter your lawn or garden size first, since it's what enables the carry-over button later.
  • Type in every value from your physical report exactly as printed — P, K, Ca, Mg, micronutrients, pH, organic matter, salts, the lime recommendation — and an interpretation card appears for each one.
  • Once you've entered your values, click "Continue to [X] Calculator" to jump to your calculator tab (it appears automatically, scrolled to the top) with everything carried over.
2
Have more than one area? A lawn, a vegetable bed, a flower bed, and a shrub bed each need their own soil sample and their own report. Come back to this tab, enter the next report, and the calculator tab bar switches to match it.
3
Retest after three years. All fertilizer and lime recommendations are valid for up to three growing seasons.

What's different once you're on your tab:

1. Enter your lawn size.

2. Choose your grass species — sets the nitrogen ceiling and per-application limit for that species.

3–5. Enter your N recommendation, P rating, and K rating from your report.

6. Enter your lime recommendation, lime type, CCE %, and bag size — the calculator tells you how many bags you need and how many applications to split it into. Lime is calculated right here, not on a separate tab.

7. Build your application plan: add up to 4 application slots, each with its own N-P-K grade and WIN% (see "Understanding WIN & Nitrogen Programs" below for what WIN% means and why it changes your per-application limit).

8. Check the Season Summary — it tracks your cumulative N, P, and K against the soil test recommendation as you add slots.

9. Mulch-mowing and returning clippings? Reduce your total annual nitrogen by up to a third. (VCE 430-011)

Works the same way as Cool-Season Lawns above:

1. Enter your lawn size.

2. Choose your warm-season species — bermudagrass and St. Augustine grass tolerate a higher per-application rate than cool-season species do.

3–5. Enter your N recommendation, P rating, and K rating from your report.

6. Enter your lime recommendation, lime type, CCE %, and bag size — same inline lime math as Cool-Season, right on this tab.

7. Add up to 4 application slots with N-P-K grade and WIN%.

8. Check the cumulative Season Summary.

🧬 Understanding WIN & Nitrogen Programs (Lawn tabs only)

This section applies to the Cool-Season and Warm-Season Lawn tabs only — it doesn't apply to Vegetable Garden, Flower Garden, or Shrubs & Trees. The nitrogen source in your fertilizer affects how it should be applied. This calculator uses the three-program system defined in VCE Publication 430-011 and Soil Test Notes 17 and 18.

Program 1 — Quickly Available Nitrogen

Fertilizers with less than 15% WIN. Includes urea, ammonium sulfate, and calcium nitrate.

  • Cool-season max: 0.7 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
  • Warm-season max: 0.9 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
Program 2 — Slowly Available Nitrogen

Fertilizers with 15–49% WIN. Includes urea formaldehyde, sulfur-coated urea, polymer-coated urea, IBDU, and natural organics.

  • Cool-season max: 0.9 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
  • Warm-season max: 1.0 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
Program 3 — Majority Slowly Available Nitrogen

Fertilizers with 50% or more WIN. Includes highly processed slow-release products and most natural organics.

  • Cool-season max: 1.0–1.5 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
  • Warm-season max: 1.0–1.5 lb N / 1,000 sq. ft. per application
  • Lower seasonal totals apply (2.0–2.8 lbs cool-season) due to higher per-application rates

Note: Sulfur-coated and polymer-coated urea fertilizers provide slowly available N but the bag label does not list it as WIN. If the fertilizer contains sulfur-coated urea, count that portion as WIN when determining your program. (VCE 430-011)

🧪 Single-Nutrient Amendment Philosophy (Vegetable Garden tab)

The Vegetable Garden tab doesn't recommend one blended N-P-K fertilizer for the whole bed. Instead, it looks at each nutrient individually and only suggests adding what's actually low — the reasoning behind that, and how a few related pieces of the tab work, is explained here.

Why single-nutrient instead of blended

A blended fertilizer sized to correct a nitrogen shortfall will also add whatever phosphorus and potassium happen to be in that bag — even if your soil test already rates P or K High or Very High. Over-applying P in particular is a real environmental concern (runoff into waterways). This tab follows VCE Soil Test Note 19's own approach: address each nutrient that's rated Low on its own, with its own product and its own rate, so you're never forced to over-apply one nutrient to correct another.

Organic vs. synthetic choice

Wherever both a plausible organic and synthetic source exist for a nutrient, the results panel offers a dropdown to choose between them instead of picking one for you — for example Bone Meal or Rock Phosphate (organic) vs. Triple Superphosphate (synthetic) for phosphorus, or Blood Meal and Fish Meal (organic) vs. Calcium Nitrate and Urea (synthetic) for nitrogen. Each option's note explains its release speed and any special handling.

Waypoint numeric target vs. VCE rating-based rate

Waypoint reports print an exact numeric target for most nutrients (e.g., "0.13 lbs S per 1,000 sq. ft.") in their "SOIL FERTILITY GUIDELINES" table. Enter that number in the nutrient's own row in the results for a precise amount sized to your bed. VCE reports don't print an equivalent number, so for those the calculator falls back to VCE Soil Test Note 19's rating-based flat rate instead (e.g., 10 lbs of bone meal per 100 sq. ft. when P rates Medium or Low). One nuance worth knowing: Waypoint's numeric target is a maintenance figure, not just a deficiency flag — it's sometimes printed even when the nutrient is rated adequate. When that happens, this tab still shows the amount for that target rather than hiding it just because the rating alone would say "no addition needed."

Bulk density input for volume estimates

Several organic amendments (blood meal, feather meal, bone meal, and others) don't have a bulk-density figure from a source that meets this tool's sourcing standard, so by default the calculator shows the weight only and tells you to weigh the product rather than measure it by volume. If you have the bulk density for your specific product — from the label, bag, or manufacturer's technical data sheet — you can enter it (in lbs/ft³ or kg/m³) for a volume estimate, clearly labeled as coming from your entry. Note that MSDS/SDS sheets often list a specific gravity that reflects the pure crystal or absolute compound density, not the packaged granular form — for example, Sulfate of Potash SDS lists SG 2.66 (equivalent to 166 lbs/ft³ absolute crystal density) while the actual granular product is 75–81 lbs/ft³ loose. Entering an SDS specific gravity would produce a volume estimate roughly half the correct size, which is why specific gravity is not accepted as an input here.

Why this level of detail

The goal throughout is to never show a number that isn't traceable to a real source — VCE, another university Extension, a government agency, or a named peer-reviewed publication — and to say clearly whenever something is an estimate rather than a verified fact, rather than presenting every number with the same false confidence.

🔬 Soil Test Ratings Explained

Ratings reflect the relative availability of nutrients in your soil. VCE / Virginia Tech and Waypoint Analytical use different scales, but they are directly comparable — this calculator maps them automatically when you select your report type.

VCE / Virginia Tech scale (Soil Test Note 1, VCE 452-701)

RatingMeaningFertilizer Response
L-, L, L+LowPlants almost always respond. Use fertilizer containing that nutrient.
M-, M, M+MediumPlants sometimes respond. Moderate fertilizer recommended.
H-, H, H+HighPlants usually do not respond. Zero P or zero K fertilizer recommended.
VHVery HighNo response expected. Use nitrogen-only fertilizer; retest next year.

Waypoint Analytical scale (Agronomy Facts 8, Waypoint Analytical 2023)

RatingEquivalent VCE ratingFertilizer Response
Very LowLPlants almost always respond. Use fertilizer containing that nutrient.
LowL / L+Plants likely respond. Use fertilizer containing that nutrient.
MediumMPlants sometimes respond. Moderate fertilizer recommended.
OptimumH-Soil supply is adequate. Maintenance applications only.
Very HighVHNo response expected. Use nitrogen-only fertilizer.

VCE medium phosphorus = 12–35 lbs/A. Medium potassium = 76–175 lbs/A. Waypoint reports nutrients in ppm; VCE reports in lbs/A. The two labs use different extraction methods, but ratings have been calibrated to be directly comparable for Virginia soils. This calculator maps Waypoint ratings to VCE equivalents automatically when you select your report type.
